# Chapter 6 — When Answers Deserve Doubt

Every prompting guide tells you to trust but verify; almost none tells you how
to *actually* verify. This chapter does. Here's the honest frame first: a model
predicts likely-sounding text, which is usually what you want and occasionally
exactly what you don't. Your job isn't to be paranoid, or credulous, but to
develop a librarian's reflex — a split-second reader's hesitation about
anything from context (or retrieval).

## The four signs an answer was written from memory, not from your quotes

1. **No source path.** Your documents were all indexed with file names (Chapters
   2–4). An answer about "your files" that cites none is written from the
   model's general knowledge, or from a feel of your pile's *shape* — not your
   pile.
2. **Perfectly polished numbers.** Real-world documents are ragged: dates that
   don't align, amounts with typos, thought-fragments. A suspiciously smooth
   amount or timeline that your actual files contradict is a red flag.
3. **Unfamiliar phrasing.** Your insurance letters say "explanation of
   benefits." History says "EOB." If the answer scrambles these into something
   you've never seen in your pile, ask where that language came from.
4. **Too-complete story.** Your life's documents have gaps. When an answer
   offers total, gap-free coverage — every date sorted, every vignette
   seamless — that's the "decorating" habit from Chapter 1, not your literal
   paper trail.

## Quick verification moves

- **Ask for the source file.** "Which document is that from?" is free. Then
  (this part matters) *actually open the file* and check it. The AI's job is
  discovery; yours is the last read.
- **Search the word it introduced.** Ctrl+F the exact term in your top-level
  ledger, or grep the whole pile. Not there? Dead giveaway.
- **Ask again smaller.** Ask your follow-up in a fresh chat with the single
  relevant paragraph pasted. If the small-chat answer contradicts the
  big-answer, go with the small one.
- **Ask it to argue the negative.** "What in my file pile *contradicts* this?"
  An answer that can't find counterbalance in your own record is often just so.

## The stakes map — where doubt matters more

Not all questions weigh the same. A map of stakes:

| Stakes | Example | Doubt level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fun work (drafting, summarizing, finding) | "Find that chapter five quote" | Quote check, and done |
| Money moves (billing, refunds, filing) | "How much do I owe for Feb?" | Open the document; verify |
| Legal or government | "Does this notice need a reply by Friday?" | Verify — then a pro |
| Health, yours or others' | "Why did my visiting hours change between these treatments?" | Verify documents for *what was written* — never for *what to do medically* |

Read that last row carefully, because it's the caution worth tattooing: an AI
can be excellent at telling you **what your documents say** and have no right
to say **what to do about them.** "My bill says X" is a document skill — fair
game. "Therefore don't get Y treatment" is medical advice; wherever that line
lives for you, keep it well left of a chat window.

This applies to the tools, too: the quote-file tool from Chapter 3 is a
*finding* device. It surfaces quotes; it never links them to what you should
do. That division of labor is exactly the design doing its job.

## Grayscale: what "doubt" is

It's worth saying what healthy doubt is *not*: a thrown-hands "AI lies!"
Nothing here means AI answers will be wrong. Most are right, and verified ones
are right so often it's disorienting. The healthy stance isn't "don't believe
it," but "don't believe it *unverified*." You'd do that with a friend's memory
of a bill ("did I pay that? — check"), and the AI deserves (and gets better
from) the same generosity.

## When to stop asking the AI

Old question, still true: when the next step stops being "find what my files
say" and starts being "decide what to do," that's the moment to carry your
quotes to a human professional — a doctor, a lawyer, your cooperative
extension, your accountant — and this guide's techniques have earned nothing
but applause for getting you there faster, with the specific, sourced,
already-found documents in your hand.

## In one sentence

> Ask what your documents say, verify the quote it came from, and never outsource
> a decision that needs a professional's license or your real judgment.
