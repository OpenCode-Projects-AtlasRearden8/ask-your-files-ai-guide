Chapter 9 — Glossary

Every term used in this manual, explained like a friend would — casually, accurately, and in one or two sentences each.

Chunk — A piece of a document, sized to be quotable. In this guide, chapters of your pile get chopped into paragraph-sized pieces, and each piece is one "chunk." Chunking is where a RAG system's quality starts: wrong-sized chunks mean ambiguous quotes.

Context window — An AI model's working desk. Everything the model can see for one reply — your prompt, pasted documents, the conversation so far — must fit in this memory. Pasting your whole library eventually can't fit, which by Chapter 1 is why we retrieve.

Embedding — A way of turning text into a list of numbers such that similar-meaning texts land near each other. The local model nomic-embed-text (Chapter 3, with Ollama) makes these embeddings on your machine; scoring a question against quotes becomes a fast similarity comparison.

Folder packing — Bundling a folder's files into one prompt, each file separated by a header (Chapter 4). Great for cross-file questions over small piles; skip it when the pile is big — that's a quote-file problem.

Hallucination — When an AI states something that isn't true or isn't in your documents, in fluent confident prose. Not lying exactly — more decorating (Chapter 1). The cure you own: quotes, visible sources, verification (Chapter 6).

Index (document index) — A helper file that says what your pile contains. INDEX.md is your hand-made one (Chapter 2); quotes.json is the tool-built one (Chapter 3) — a list of chunks with their source files.

JSON — A plain-text structured data format, openable in any editor. Your quote file is JSON on purpose: no database to trust, no binary to damage, easy to back up, diff, and delete.

Ledger — This guide's nickname for the one-file-per-folder index (INDEX.md from Chapter 2: date, filename, subject, tags — one line each).

Local-first — Designing so everything runs on your computer: no uploads, no accounts, no retention settings to trust, no data leaving your machine except when you deliberately paste (Chapter 5's Rule 1).

Lost-in-the-middle — A documented behavior where models pay less attention to the middle of very long inputs. Important only because it's invisible: pastes can silently miss detail while sounding confident.

Ollama — Free software that runs open AI models on your own computer, locally. In this guide it optionally provides embeddings (nomic-embed-text) and local answering (llama3.2) for the quote-file tool.

Quote file — The JSON index this guide builds. Pulls the quotable chunks of your files — hence the file's function: a searchable shelf of quotable pieces.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) — Ask-anything pattern: retrieve the small relevant pieces of your documents first, then generate an answer with those pieces visibly in the prompt. This entire guide is one friendly version of it.

Redaction — Replacing private details (names, numbers, addresses) with placeholders before pasting. Habit, not event — keep placeholder text handy (Chapter 5).

Retrieval — Finding the right piece of your pile for a question, before any generation happens. Chapter 2's search, Chapter 3's quote file, and Chapter 4's packing are all retrieval methods at different rungs of effort.

Scoring — Giving each quote a number for "how relevant to this question?" With TF-IDF it's word overlap math; with embeddings it's meaning-space distance; with your eyes it's --top 20 and a skim.

Source path — The file a quote came from (docs/eob-2024-02-11.txt). The core safety feature of the whole pipeline: every answer should be traceable to one. Chapter 6's first verification move is always "which file?"

TF-IDF — The classic word-overlap scoring method built into the quote-file tool as its offline default: terms counted per quote scaled by how rare they are across your pile ("CO-97" beats "claim"). No model, no download, works anywhere Python runs.

Tag — A short word you deliberately associate with files (shown in your ledger as tags: ... in Chapter 2). Cheap and hand-made; they make search specific and give the chapter-3 index the vocabulary of your pile.

Token — The units models read and write, roughly word-chunks. Context windows are measured in thousands of tokens; your pastes are too. Why pasting fails at scale is simply that tokens in documents outnumber tokens on the desk.

Topical outline — A bulleted map of your pile's themes (OUTLINE.md in Chapter 2). Answers questions by itself, seeds your tags, and doubles as the cover sheet pasted at the top of any AI session.

Verification (closing the loop) — The one claim, one source-file, one real read (Chapter 6). The habit that turns an AI from a plausible-sounding stranger into a useful research assistant.

That's the whole book. If you made it here having run your first quote-file ask and checked one source quote, you have the skill this manual exists to teach. Everything else is practice — and Chapter 7 will be waiting the next time a new folder shows up in your life.
