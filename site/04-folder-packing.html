Chapter 4 — The Folder-Packing Approach

Sometimes the honest answer is: you genuinely need the whole folder at once. A cross-document question — "bottlecap chapter appears in which of my drafts?" "which letters ever mention June 14th?" — can't be answered from one quote at a time. Folder packing is the technique: one script turns a folder into one prompt, everything dated, named, and compact.

This chapter tells you when to pack, how to pack well, and how the companion tool folder-to-prompt-cli fits.

When packing is the right call

Pack when the question spans files rather than living inside one:

  • Inventories — "list every claim amount across all replies."
  • Name-driven maps — "which files mention X?" (then Chapter 2 search. Packing finds fuzzy as well as exact matches.)
  • Small piles that fit. If your folder has ~15 short files, packing and asking usually beats building an index.

Skip packing when the pile is long, is mostly boilerplate you'll never need, or contains anything too personal to paste (Chapter 5 — loud).

How a good pack looks

A good pack is a plain-text bundle, files separated by metadata headers so the model (and you, months later) can trace any line to its source:

=== FILE: sample-docs/eob-2024-02-11.txt (11 Feb 2024, 412 chars) ===
First paragraph of my explanation-of-benefits letter...
Second paragraph...

=== FILE: sample-docs/eob-2024-03-05.txt (5 Mar 2025, 380 chars) ===
...

Why this style works better than cat *.txt > one.txt:

  • Clear fences. Models treat === FILE: markers as boundaries, and the AI can mention files by name in its answer.
  • Sizes visible. A 400-char file is skimmable; knowing size bounds the model's guess about what's worth opening.
  • Dates resolve "which one was newest?" in one step.

The workflow, end to end

Our guide ships with a minimal packer below you can type in ten minutes, and the companion tool folder-to-prompt-cli automates the polished version (extensions filtering, file-type detection, redaction lists, clipboard copy): one command, your whole folder in one prompt, ready to paste or file.

# with the companion tool installed:
folder-to-prompt ./my-docs --out packed-prompt.txt --ask "$(cat question.txt)"

A minimal packer you can own

No companion tool? Here's the same idea in ~12 lines of Python you can put in your own bin folder, named pack.py:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""pack.py — bundle a folder of .txt/.md files into one prompt."""
import sys, pathlib

folder, out = pathlib.Path(sys.argv[1]), sys.argv[2]
parts = []
for f in sorted(p for p in folder.rglob("*") if p.suffix.lower() in {".txt", ".md"}):
    text = f.read_text(encoding="utf-8", errors="replace").strip()
    rel = f.relative_to(folder)
    parts.append(f"=== FILE: {rel} ({len(text)} chars) ===\n{text}\n")
pathlib.Path(out).write_text("\n".join(parts), encoding="utf-8")
print(f"Packed {len(parts)} files -> {out} "
      f"({sum(len(p) for p in parts)} chars total)")
python3 pack.py sample-docs packed-prompt.txt

Read what it does: it walks the folder, keeps only the extensions you care about, and writes one header per file. That's it — no network, no magic. Paste the output into your next chat as one message.

The packer's guardrails (small, and worth keeping)

  1. Exclude before you ever pack. Decide which folders are off-limits first — for most people: anything with budget, ledger, tax, or health records in the path. Your packer (and the companion tool's --exclude flag) should skip them by rule, not by memory.
  2. Keep a character budget. A pack that's too big silently reintroduces the Chapter 1 problems. If the whole bundle is over roughly 20–30k words, you don't have a packing problem — you have a quote-file problem, and Chapter 3 is your friend again.
  3. A copy stays in your folder. The pack is just another file. If you keep it (packed-prompt.txt), every question already has its file-visible source archive; save the answer next to it and you have hand-made provenance.

Where chat-export-archiver-cli fits

The same alias exists in reverse: your AI chats themselves are documents — the questions you asked and the answers you got. Saving them, trimming the noise, and accumulating them into one searchable archive turns your AI history into a first-class quote file (Chapter 3's format works great). That's exactly what the companion tool chat-export-archiver-cli does, tidily, offline. End of the loop: your questions about your files become files you can ask about.

In one sentence

When a question spans the whole pile, pack the pile into one honest, headered prompt — and keep packing as a conscious, deliberate move, not a habit.
